Case study · inclusive.io · AAA where the design holds it

The design system, and the gate that took my claim off me.

WCAG 2.2 AAA is the top accessibility standard. Material, Chakra, Mantine and Radix all stop at AA. Almost every system that claims AAA claims it in a document, because almost nothing in a normal pipeline can tell you the moment it stops being true.

So I built the thing that could. A contrast gate that models what the UI actually renders rather than what the tokens say, an axe sweep across every route in both themes, and three end-to-end specs for the criteria you can't assert your way to. The first full run was uncomfortable, which is the entire point of writing it. Twenty-five of 167 routes had ever been scanned, and the unscanned 85% was sitting on twelve unlabelled inputs and six buttons with no accessible name, which are Level A failures. I'll be fair to the libraries, because AA is a sensible default and shipping it properly is real work. But compliant ought to mean something a machine can check. At what stage does a claim nobody can reproduce stop counting as a claim?

01 · The constraints

Three constraints, and none of them were the technology.

Solo Developer

No team, nobody reviewing my PRs, and architecture and build and docs and testing all of it mine, which means every gate that didn't exist was mine as well.

AAA, Not AA

7:1 contrast, 44px targets, reduced motion treated as a requirement rather than a toggle. Every shortcut the other libraries take was off the table.

No Reference Implementation

Nothing out there ships the enhanced criteria as standard. So there wasn't a playbook to copy, and no just-do-what-Google-did to fall back on, and no worked example anywhere of how you'd go about proving any of it either.

02 · Why it got built twice

HSL cannot promise you a contrast ratio, however nicely you ask.

V1 worked and V1 shipped and people used it every day. It also had a ceiling I couldn't raise. So it got rebuilt.

Rebuilding wasn't admitting failure, it was admitting the foundation had to change, because HSL couldn't guarantee perceptual uniformity and ShadCN's styling couldn't carry the token depth the enhanced criteria need. This wasn't a bug-fixing job, it was laying something that could take the next hundred components without dropping accessibility somewhere along the way and nobody noticing for a year.

OKLCH replaced HSL so contrast is perceptually uniform, React Aria and HeroUI replaced Radix for proper keyboard and ARIA handling, and a three-tier W3C DTCG token system replaced the flat CSS variables. Then the audit found two colour systems living in the same repository. Two. The specifications were documenting the one that didn't paint the UI, and 1,157 Tailwind palette utilities across 205 files were bypassing the tokens entirely. Migrating those is most of what the last month actually was.

ShadCN was quick to move into and it was good for settling the patterns, and then it hit a ceiling, because HSL made the contrast maths unreliable and the flat token structure couldn't carry the semantic layering that enhanced contrast needs and Radix stops at AA by design. None of that's a criticism of the tools, they're good tools. It's what happens when you keep asking a foundation to do a job it was never scoped for.

Rebuilding something that already works takes more nerve than starting fresh, because you have to say out loud, to people who can see it working, that the foundation won't take what's coming next.

04 · How it was decided

Three methods, run in parallel, each made to return evidence.

Before any of it was rebuilt, it was investigated. Three methods, run in parallel, each required to return evidence rather than impressions.

Forensic internal audit

The codebase read dimension by dimension, which meant types and the build pipeline, then tokens, then tests, then performance, then accessibility, then hygiene, and every finding had to name a file and a line before it counted as a finding at all. Every count in this case study comes off that pass. None of it is memory.

External benchmarking

Twelve products read against the two surfaces this system has to compete with. Five recruitment platforms for the ATS, seven design systems for the documentation.

Standards review

WCAG 2.2 and the DTCG Format Module 2025.10 read directly, rather than relying on folklore about what either requires. The token architecture follows the specification that stabilised in October 2025.

05 · The calls

Four decisions, and what each one cost.

  1. Say AA, earn AAA

    Stop making a blanket AAA claim the evidence didn't support.

    The system was advertising AAA while shipping text that failed AA. The honest position is AA as a non-negotiable baseline, AAA where the visual language genuinely allows it, and the exceptions published rather than omitted.

  2. Do not fabricate the demo data

    Label demo data as demo data, and make the interactions believable client-side instead.

    An ATS demo shows diversity and pipeline metrics. Inventing numbers there isn't a harmless placeholder. It's fabricated D&I reporting, on exactly the content where a reader is most entitled to trust what they see.

  3. Migrate colour with the redesign, not before it

    Fold roughly 1,751 hardcoded palette utilities into the redesign rather than sweeping them mechanically first.

    A blind sweep would have touched every screen with no visual review and no way to tell a fix from a regression. Doing it behind semantic tokens during the redesign meant the visual work and the token work landed together, verified together.

  4. Diagnose before prescribing

    Treat 2,430 TypeScript errors as a symptom before treating them as a workload.

    The obvious reading was an untyped library needing a rewrite. It was a publishing defect: the library shipped no declaration files, so every consumer silently fell back to bundled JavaScript and every component's props collapsed to any. Emitting declarations took it to 599. A single directive in a service worker, stripping the DOM lib from the whole program, accounted for the rest.

06 · The verification pass

Twelve findings, and the claim came off me.

Every claim on this page had to survive a pass that treated it as an unverified assertion, which meant nothing got restated from an earlier document and every verdict had to come back from a command's output, or from a file and a line number, before it was allowed to stay on the page. Nothing here is remembered.

Read the full account of the audit

Twelve findings came back. The contrast gate got rebuilt to model what the UI actually does, which is a tint on a card on a card, and it immediately failed six text tokens sitting at 6.71 to 6.85:1 that a flat model had waved straight through. Route coverage went from fifteen percent to all 174. Nine defects surfaced only because a gate had been written to look for them. An unnamed dialog behind every sheet in the system. A menu marking the current page by colour alone. A rich-text toolbar whose active states had never once worked, and every skip link in the product rendering as an invisible 1x1 box. You don't find those by reading the code. You find them by writing something that can fail you.

Writing the gate is the work. A standard you can only assert is a standard you haven't met yet, and you won't know which until somebody checks.

07 · What it proves

AA everywhere, gated. AAA on thirteen criteria, and three exceptions published.

AAA at scale is doable as the default rather than a nice-to-have you reach if there's time left over. The proof isn't that I say so. It's 1,564 tests you can run yourself.
