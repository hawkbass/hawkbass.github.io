The thing I keep being hired to fix

We went from 200kbps to 2000mbps, everything got fancier, usability got cluttered and overcomplicated.

Three decades on from documents anybody could open, it takes two megabytes of JavaScript to show you a paragraph that was already text. The scroll gets hijacked so a logo can rotate on the way past. Zoom gets switched off because it breaks the layout. There's a functioning industry selling a widget that makes an inaccessible site compliant without making it one bit easier to read.

Machines got faster every single year of that, and the whole gain went on decoration. Almost none of it is malicious, because checking who got locked out is boring and the cool shit is what gets shared. But the outcome stops caring what you meant. Somebody can't read the page, the reason is a contrast ratio that looked nicer, and your information is now behind a door with you holding the key.

Preloaders and hamburger menus can fuck all the way off and stay there.

Preloaders
Not skeletons. A skeleton's the page telling you what's arriving while it fetches it, and that's honest. I mean the preloader, a gate somebody's deliberately put in front of a page that was ready before it started, so you can sit and watch a bar fill and think about what you've done. Same instinct as everything above. The information isn't the point, looking clever on the way to it is. They were ridiculous in 2004 running Flash on Internet Explorer. Building one on purpose in 2026 makes me want to chew my own lips off, and if I've to sit through one on a gigabit connection I'm going to assume you're an idiot.
Hamburger menus
They turned up as the quickest way to make a menu work on a phone and then stuck around being shit for a decade or more, purely because everybody else does it and nobody wanted to be the one who stopped. The navigation goes behind a click for no reason anyone can defend out loud. They were shit then, they're shit now, and if you use one I'll probably tell you so directly.
Theme parks are made for people, but not disabled people. That is slowly changing. The web is going the other way, and it has better tools and more bandwidth than it has ever had.

Nobody sets out to lock people out. It just never gets checked, and unchecked always drifts one way.

The whole job

95% + 5% = 100%

The web needs to be for everyone. Not the 95%. All of it. That is the entire job and it is not complicated, it is just unglamorous.

Five people in my family and I'm the only one who isn't dyslexic. One of my sisters has never once mentioned it and just reads the paragraph twice.

The British Dyslexia Association asks for dark text on a light background rather than black on pure white, because white dazzles. WCAG pushes the contrast up, the dyslexia guidance pushes it back down, the maximum turns out not to be the target, and somebody has to sit and work out which reader they are serving. That is a person's job, not a linter's. The ground on this page is not white.

How I'd change what you ship

A statement is not a standard.

A standard you can only assert is a standard you haven't met yet, and you won't know which until something checks.

Nearly every company shipping an inaccessible site has an accessibility statement somewhere on it. Almost none have a test that could fail. A statement costs an afternoon. A gate costs you the argument, in public, the day it goes red, which is exactly why it works and exactly why nobody wants to write it.

Writing the gate is the work. I put one in front of my own AAA claim and it took the claim straight off me: nine real defects nothing else had caught, including an unnamed dialog sitting behind every sheet in the system. That is the version of this you actually want on your team.

Shipped as gates, not intentions: 360 contrast pairings on every run · 1,564 tests across 174 routes · 0 axe violations in both themes · AA everywhere, AAA on thirteen criteria, three exceptions published rather than quietly dropped · a colour-literal ratchet that only moves down · a check on the delivered CSS rather than the source, because the build was quietly flattening OKLCH to hex and nothing said a word.

Here's how this site is put together, while you're on it.

Tokens
Warm OKLCH palette that actually ships as OKLCH. The build was quietly flattening the lot of it to sRGB hex to keep Opera Mini happy, and nothing said a word about it, so there's a gate on the delivered CSS now rather than the source it came from.
Gates
56 colour pairings checked on every run, 309 end-to-end tests across every route, both modes and three browser engines, and a ratchet on raw colour literals that's only ever allowed to go down. 53 literals still bypass the theme, and that number's on screen because hiding it would be the entire problem. Run npm run verify and watch it for yourself.
Platform
Three layouts now, and it's been both one and three before this. The first three were picked by viewport width after the page had loaded, so every section was a client component, nothing could be server-rendered, and a phone sat through 310ms of blocking time before it could do a thing. So I went to one fluid build, which was fast, and on an actual phone it looked horrific. This time the device is stamped on the page before the first paint, the sections are one copy of the argument that each layout arranges for its own screen, and the only separate code is the navigation, so the phone gets its own layout without paying for it in blocking time. No user-agent string was consulted at any point, then or now. They lie, and they always have.
Motion
Settles before the first paint and respects reduced-motion. Scroll reveals animate transform and nothing else. Fade the text instead and it'll spend the whole journey under its own contrast threshold.
Writing
Notes on what the gates found, including the eight months I claimed AAA while the axe suite had never once loaded the 7:1 rule.
Documentation
The whole build written up at /build, including what a full audit of it found. Fourteen findings, nine fixed the same day, and two of them level A accessibility failures that'd been sitting live for months because the test suite only ever ran at one screen width. Read it at /build
Proof
inclusive.io, Quoin, the payment work and this site all run the same discipline: build the thing, then go and build the thing that can prove it wrong.

This is the bit that makes people go quiet in interviews. Not the number, the fact that it can fail.

Why this pays for itself

The accessibility fix and the money fix keep turning out to be the same fix.

A person in arrears, on a phone, being asked to type a sixteen digit card number into a debt collection form has several good reasons to give up. Removing those reasons is an accessibility job and a conversion job at the same time. Mobile payments went up 80%, and 70% of them came through Apple Pay.

That flow had four screens because those two jobs belonged to two different people. Almost every expensive problem I get called into lives in that gap. Design says it's an engineering problem, engineering says it's a design one, and it sits there for a year with nobody's name against it. I can hold both halves, which is the only reason I can see it at all.

SagePay to Fiserv, finished solo after roughly half a dozen developers had left it short of testing: tokenisation, digital wallets, HMAC-SHA256, 3D Secure, store ID routing, 2,046 lines of C# I hadn't written since 2016, against a PCI-DSS estate of 97+ stored procedures that never stopped taking live money. Zero failed cutovers.

Nobody's posting a Fiserv gateway on Dribbble with a nice gradient behind it. It still moved more money than anything I've made.

How I got here

You can't see a foundation. You only ever see what happens when there wasn't one.

I started building for the web at fourteen and had the degree by nineteen. Then I couldn't get a job doing it. A few years of freelance and whatever else paid, by the end of which I'd had enough of the industry entirely, so I retrained as a graphic designer and did that properly for six years instead. Print doesn't let you away with anything. One shot, it goes on a press, and if the bleed's wrong you find out in a room full of boxes. I came back to code in 2020 and spent the guts of a year rebuilding everything I'd let go rusty. So this isn't a designer who learned to build, it's the other way round, and the six years in the middle are the whole point. I make shit look good, and then I build it, because I spent far too long not trusting anybody else to do a decent job of either half.

Six years at NOVAVAPES, and the reason I know what design costs when you get it wrong. Graphic designer up to running the brand outright, doing packaging, stands, merch and every other touchpoint with nobody else to check a bit of it. That teaches you quicker than anything that a design decision doesn't sit still, it compounds while you aren't looking. A weak logo becomes weak packaging, becomes a shelf nobody reaches for, and by then it's a print run you can't unprint and a warehouse you're paying rent on. The one I'd still put in front of anybody is the stand for the biggest vape expo in Europe, which took the best part of two years. Standing in a room being walked through it by people who'd no idea I was watching them is a strange way to find out whether the work landed.

DesignBuddy has run since 2017. 50+ clients, brand and e-commerce and web, national ad campaigns. The bit nobody warns you about when you go out on your own is that you pitch it and you win it and you build it, and then your phone goes at half seven on a Sunday because something's fallen over and there is nobody else. Some of those years went past like weeks, some of those weeks went past like years. Still going after nearly a decade, which for a one-person studio is the only review that counts. Earlier this year I put together a full redesign proposal for Dundonald Castle that nobody asked for and nobody paid for, because the site wasn't doing the place any justice and I couldn't leave it alone. That is more or less the whole story of how I pick what to work on.

I co-founded inclusive.io and built the 117-component system myself, twice, because the first one ran on HSL and HSL can't promise you a contrast ratio no matter how nicely you ask it. The whole foundation came out and went back in as OKLCH with three-tier W3C tokens, 26 ATS workflows and React Aria underneath, holding from a Galaxy Fold shut all the way out to a 5K ultrawide. It's the best thing I've built. Then I pointed the verification at my own accessibility claim and it took the claim straight back off me, and that went into the case study rather than getting quietly deleted, because a portfolio that only shows the bits that worked is a showreel.

Most recently, payment infrastructure at BPO Collections, which finished in April. I was brought in for an accessibility rebuild and then asked whether I'd take the SagePay to Fiserv migration instead, which roughly half a dozen developers had already had a go at and not one of them had taken as far as testing. I finished it on my own: Fiserv end to end, tokenisation, 3D Secure, a legacy ASP.NET estate hauled onto typed Next.js, and a PCI-DSS database where there's no such thing as roughly right. It's the least fashionable work I've done and easily the most useful, which took me a while to say out loud, because when it breaks it isn't an ugly page. It's somebody already in arrears who can't get the payment through.

AI & Agent Systems

I've been paying attention since the transformer paper in 2017, which is a long time to find something interesting before it's any use to anybody at all. These days it's OpenAI and Anthropic most hours of the day, and Cursor for the fiddly bits. It changed what one careful person gets through in a week, it did nothing for careless people. That's most of why half the internet now reads like a leaflet somebody put through your door.

The speed is real and so is the rubbish, and the difference between them is entirely whether somebody read the thing before it went out. I run agent sessions the way I run builds. Tight brief, obvious done-state, nothing ships that I haven't read end to end. The skills and MCP servers and rules sitting in this repo are there because without them the work wanders off and comes back sounding like everybody else. Which is the one thing I'm not interested in being.

Half the setup is on this site if you want to poke at it, so hit Ctrl and full stop for System Mode, or go and read llms.txt, and it's the same desk behind inclusive.io and the payment work and this portfolio. Everything on it gets checked like production code before it goes anywhere near a user, and I'll tell you exactly which parts the machine wrote.
